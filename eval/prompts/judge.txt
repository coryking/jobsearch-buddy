You are evaluating how well a job description was stripped for vector search.

WHAT YOU'RE LOOKING AT: One original job description and its stripped version.
You will judge many such pairs across diverse companies, industries, and roles —
defense contractors, AI startups, fintech, enterprise SaaS, government, and more.
Apply the same rubric consistently regardless of industry or role type.

WHY WE STRIP: The stripped text becomes a single vector in a search index
containing thousands of job postings from 100+ different companies. Users
search across ALL companies with natural language queries like "robotics
engineer with security clearance near DC", "PM roles at companies working
on internet privacy", or "senior backend engineer at AI startup." Every
word in the stripped text influences the vector.

Text that appears identically across thousands of postings — EEO statements,
generic benefits, legal disclaimers — pushes every job's vector toward the same
point, drowning out the signal that makes each job unique. That shared text
must be removed. But content specific to THIS company or THIS role is the
signal that helps the right job surface for the right query — it must stay.

THE OPERATION IS DELETION ONLY: The strip model removes content from the
original. It should never rewrite, paraphrase, summarize, or add text.

WHAT SHOULD BE REMOVED (interchangeable across companies' postings):
- EEO / equal opportunity statements
- Generic benefits lists (the standard health/dental/vision/401k/PTO package)
- Drug-free workplace policies
- Accessibility / accommodation request instructions
- Legal disclaimers, privacy policy links, applicant data notices
- Recruiter scam warnings
- "We encourage all candidates to apply" diversity encouragement paragraphs

WHAT SHOULD BE KEPT (differentiates this posting in cross-company search):
- Role responsibilities, qualifications, tech stack, team/project descriptions
- Company description, mission, products, named projects — anything specific
  to THIS company that wouldn't appear on other companies' postings. Example:
  Cloudflare's "Project Galileo" or Anthropic's research directions are
  company-differentiating content, not boilerplate
- Compensation details: salary ranges, equity, RSUs, signing bonuses
- Remote / hybrid / onsite details, location, reporting structure
- Eligibility requirements: export control, citizenship, security clearance,
  background checks, work authorization — these are hard gates that determine
  who can apply, not legal boilerplate. A user searching "jobs requiring
  clearance" or "roles open to non-US-citizens" needs this in the vector
- Benefits that are specific or unusual (100% coverage, fertility benefits,
  specific dollar amounts) — only the generic "standard package" list should go

THE KEY TEST: Would you find nearly identical text on thousands of other
companies' postings? If yes, remove it. If it's specific to this company
or role, keep it.

Score the stripped version on four dimensions (1-5 each):

RECALL — Did all differentiating content survive?
  This is the most important dimension. Information loss means the job won't
  surface for queries it should match.

  5: Zero differentiating content removed. Every responsibility, qualification,
     company detail, salary figure, eligibility requirement, and location detail
     from the original is present in the stripped version.
  4: One minor item lost — e.g., a single preferred qualification, a hashtag
     like #LI-Remote, or a "nice to have" bullet. Nothing that would change
     which queries match this job.
  3: Two to three meaningful details lost — e.g., a work schedule, a few
     qualifications, a nice-to-have section, or a company project description.
     The job would still surface for most relevant queries but misses some.
  2: An important category of content removed — e.g., the salary range, an
     eligibility/clearance requirement, multiple required qualifications, or
     a company-specific section (products, projects, mission). The job will
     fail to surface for a class of queries it should match.
  1: Core content gutted — the responsibilities section, the qualifications
     section, or multiple important categories are missing. The stripped
     version is missing what fundamentally defines this job.

PRECISION — Was boilerplate actually removed?
  Less critical than recall — noise hurts ranking but doesn't cause total misses.

  5: No boilerplate remains. Every sentence in the stripped version is
     differentiating content.
  4: One or two boilerplate sentences remain — e.g., a stray EEO line,
     an orphaned "we are an equal opportunity employer", a privacy link.
  3: A full boilerplate paragraph or section remains — e.g., the entire
     EEO block, the full generic benefits list, or a complete accommodation
     statement. But most boilerplate was removed.
  2: Multiple boilerplate sections remain — e.g., EEO + generic benefits,
     or EEO + accommodation + diversity encouragement. More noise than signal
     was left behind.
  1: Little to no boilerplate removed. The stripped version is essentially
     identical to the original — the model failed to perform the task.

INTEGRITY — Was any false signal introduced?
  Fabricated content poisons the search index — the job surfaces for wrong queries.

  5: Every sentence in the stripped version exists in the original. No
     additions, no rewording, no invented content of any kind.
  4: No new factual claims. At most a trivial artifact from deletion — e.g.,
     an orphaned section header with no content below it, or a dangling
     bullet point.
  3: Some phrases were reworded in ways that preserve the facts but change
     the surface form — e.g., "5+ years" became "five or more years", or
     a sentence was lightly restructured. No invented facts.
  2: New factual claims are present that weren't in the original — e.g., a
     salary range that differs from the original, a qualification that was
     strengthened ("experience with" became "expertise in"), or a location/
     requirement that was added. OR pervasive rewording that shifts what
     queries would match.
  1: Clearly fabricated content — invented salary figures, made-up
     requirements, fictional company details, or a substantially
     hallucinated section.

FIDELITY — How closely does kept text match the original surface form?
  Least important for search quality, but measures instruction-following.
  This dimension is about surface form only — if the meaning is preserved
  but words changed, that's a fidelity issue (not integrity).

  5: Character-for-character identical on all kept content. If you diff the
     stripped version against the original, the only changes are deletions.
  4: Content matches but minor formatting differences — e.g., changed
     whitespace, removed a header, converted bullets to plain text,
     or trivial punctuation changes.
  3: A few phrases reworded while fully preserving meaning — e.g.,
     "Bachelor's degree required" became "Requires a Bachelor's degree".
     Infrequent and minor.
  2: Multiple sentences reworded, restructured, or condensed. The meaning
     is mostly preserved but the text clearly went through a rewrite.
  1: Substantially rewritten. Reads like a summary or paraphrase rather
     than a subset of the original text.
